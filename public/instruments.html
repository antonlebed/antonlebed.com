Instruments

Evaluations whose best achievable score is derived rather than estimated, and standard statistical tools graded against an answer known exactly.

  • Ceilings — evaluations whose optimal score is derived, not estimated
  • Stated uncertainty — what a stated interval does when the truth is known exactly
  • Deletion audits — what published tools do when the answer is known exactly
  • Prediction sets — what a coverage guarantee leaves unscored, and what the rule built to keep it cheaply pays off equal weights
  • The swap — the identity behind the trades, the mass bound that empties the condition's below half, and the exchanges that serve an atom some optimum abandons, graded by the lightest mass

A statistical tool is ordinarily graded against an estimate. The best score any predictor could reach on a task, the Bayes-optimal score of a predictor that knows the distribution generating the data, is itself approximated from data, and whatever is measured against it inherits that approximation's error. The pages here remove the estimate. Each family of tasks is designed so that its optimum comes out in closed form, the family's ceiling, and what a tool does against a known ceiling is then a computation with an exact answer: how far its score sits from optimal, whether its stated interval covers the truth, the size of the error it cannot see. The designs run on the object the rest of these notes are about, residue arithmetic with the archimedean place deleted, where a question about size or order has an exactly computable answer and no estimate anywhere in it.

Read inward, the families chart their own dials against the ceiling: which cells of a family the evidence is worth nothing on, and how that set moves with the score asked and the prior assumed. Read outward, published tools take the same tasks — estimators of the Bayes error, deletion audits, set-valued predictors — and each miss is read as a law: the sign of the error, its rate against the sample size, or the exact limit a tool converges to instead of the truth. One law spans the section, and it is about the statement of uncertainty rather than any tool.

The sign law: a bound cannot be overconfident, a fit merely can observation

A stated interval is computed from the estimator's own model and prices only error that model has a coordinate for; error outside the model is the uncounted term. Call the statement a bound when that term has a known sign — fit a rule on half the draw, score it on the other half, state the one-sided upper limit, and the slack carried is the fitted rule's excess over the optimum, which cannot be negative — and a fit when it is a two-sided interval computed from the fitted model's own noise. On designed worlds — fifteen equally likely evidence values, each carrying a stated rate of label 1, so the best possible error is an exact rational — no world and no sample size made the bound cover below its one-sided nominal: 0.963–0.988 against 0.975 at all eight readings, the knife world included, where every rate is 1/2 exactly and the fit on the same world covers 0.015–0.035. Only that one direction is forced. A fit is merely the kind that can fail toward confidence, and nothing makes it: the knife fit's half-width runs 3.1 to 3.3 times the estimate's true dispersion, too wide rather than too narrow. Nor is a bound forced to be vacuous: this one excluded the no-evidence value, the error of the best constant answer, in every trial on the world whose rates all sit at least 1/10 from 1/2.

Scope. Observation over eight readings, the bound arm on two worlds at four sample sizes from 500 to 32,000, 400 trials each, 0-1 score, every reading inside its Monte-Carlo band. The law is measured on Bayes-error estimators; the two deletion audits scored on this section's pages both state a fit's uncertainty and fail in the direction it names. Toy scale.
