Prediction

The tower predicts primes it has never met. Build a sub-ring from a few primes. Compute its Euler characteristic. The answer names primes the sub-ring has never seen.

Formula: −χ = N × (k−1 − Σ 1/pi). For two primes: (p−1)(q−1) − 1.

The factorization rule

New predictions at rung k come from exactly two sources (proved, verified k = 3–24):

(A) Twin term: {2, pk} → pk−2. Fires when pk and pk−2 are a twin prime pair.

(B) Factorization term: each way to write (pk + 1) as (pi−1)(pj−1) with pi, pj smaller tower primes contributes one prediction.

“Rich” rungs: (pk + 1) highly composite (e.g. pk = 71, 72 = 2³·3², gains 3 new predictions). “Barren” rungs: (pk + 1) lacks usable factorizations (e.g. pk = 29, 30 = 2·3·5, gains 0).

Examples at k = 7

Four 2-prime sub-rings at k = 7 name the next prime in the chain. Each is an algebraic identity, not a numerical coincidence.

D = 2, K = 3 are the Cunningham chain starting points. Every polynomial factors as (ring element) × (D−2 or K−3).

2-invisibility (proved). The prime 2 is never named by any sub-ring that doesn't contain it. For odd-prime sub-rings, −χ is always odd. This follows from the reciprocal naming criterion: for odd primes, each 1/pi = 1 (mod 2), so the sum can never equal m−1 (mod 2).

How far the tower sees

The prediction horizon — how far ahead the tower's sub-rings can name primes — grows super-exponentially:

k=3:  horizon/pk = 5.8   k=7:  14 of 15 primes after p7 predicted   k=10:  horizon/pk = 505M

Mid-size sub-rings (~k/2 primes) are the most productive predictors. The full ring's −χ is too large (rarely prime); single primes are too small.
