The Primorial Tower

the algebra of the Eratosthenes sieve

The sieve of Eratosthenes finds primes by removing multiples: cross out multiples of 2, then 3, then 5, and so on. Run that process inside the ring Z/(2·3·5·…·pk) — the product of the first k primes — and each sieve step becomes a projection onto a prime field. The Chinese Remainder Theorem splits the ring into one independent channel per prime. The sieve, in algebraic form. That object is the primorial tower: one ring per k, each nested in the next.

This site is an exercise in minimal emergence — how much structure you can get from the fewest moving parts. The tower is constructed, not discovered: independent channels, bijective arithmetic, built-in error correction, total division, torus geometry all hold by construction, at every rung. The interesting question is never “is this surprising?” but “what can you see from here, and what can you build with it?”

What the construction gives you

Independent channels. A number's remainder mod 2 tells you nothing about its remainder mod 3, or any other prime — zero mutual information, a theorem, not an approximation. One problem with 510,510 possibilities becomes seven problems with at most 17 possibilities each.

Built-in error correction. From k = 7 onward, treat the last three channels as parity: any (k−3) of k channels reconstruct the original element. The code is maximum distance separable — no code with those parameters does better. There is no encoding layer. The ring is the code.

Prime prediction. Build a sub-ring from a few primes. Its Euler characteristic names primes the sub-ring has never met. At k = 7, fourteen of the fifteen primes after the 7th appear among the sub-ring predictions. The tower sees further as it climbs.

Things that run. The same structure works in silicon and over radio: an FPGA proving CRT arithmetic and error correction across millions of exhaustive checks, three ESP32 boards catching every injected channel error over the air, neural architectures that carry their own parity.
